You buy a house. You rent it out. The value goes up. You sell, and you walk away with a hundred grand.

That's the story people tell at dinner.

Here's the version HMRC tells: that hundred grand is a capital gain, and they want their share.

I meet a lot of UK landlords in Dubai. Many of them are thinking about the same move: sell the buy-to-let back home, bring the money to the UAE, and put it into a dollar-linked property with no annual property tax. It can be a great move. But you have to know your real number first. Not the sale price. Not the gross profit. The cash you actually keep.

So let's work it out properly.

## Key takeaways
- UK Capital Gains Tax on residential property is 18% for basic-rate taxpayers and 24% for higher and additional-rate taxpayers in 2026/27.
- The annual tax-free allowance is £3,000 per person, so £6,000 for a couple who own jointly.
- On a £100,000 gross gain with £15,000 of allowable costs, a higher-rate taxpayer pays about £19,680 and keeps about £80,320 of the profit.
- UK residents who owe CGT on a UK home or buy-to-let must report and pay within 60 days of completion.
- Moving to Dubai does not remove UK CGT on UK property. Non-residents must report every UK property sale within 60 days, even if no tax is due.

## What Capital Gains Tax actually is

Capital Gains Tax is a tax on profit. Not on the sale price. On the increase in value between what you paid and what you sold for, after allowable costs.

On property, it usually applies to:

- buy-to-let and rental properties,
- second homes and holiday lets,
- investment properties you have never lived in.

It usually does **not** apply to your main home, thanks to Private Residence Relief. More on that below, because "usually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

## The 2026/27 rates

For residential property sold by individuals in the 2026/27 tax year:

| Your band | CGT rate on the gain |
|---|---|
| Basic rate | 18% |
| Higher or additional rate | 24% |
| Annual exempt amount | £3,000 per person |

Here's the bit people miss. Your gain sits **on top of** your income for the year. If your salary or rental income already uses up most of your basic-rate band, most of your gain is taxed at 24%. If the gain straddles the line, part is taxed at 18% and part at 24%.

## Step by step: working out the tax on a rental

Let's use clean numbers.

- Bought a rental property for **£200,000** in 2021.
- Sold it in 2026 for **£300,000**.
- Gross gain: **£100,000**.

You are not taxed on the £100,000. You take things off first.

### Step 1: take off your allowable costs

HMRC lets you deduct costs directly connected with buying, selling or improving the property:

- estate agent fees when you sell,
- solicitor and conveyancing fees, buying and selling,
- Stamp Duty Land Tax you paid when you bought,
- capital improvements such as an extension, a loft conversion, or a new kitchen or bathroom that improves the property,
- costs of advertising the property for sale.

Say these come to **£15,000**.

[ILLUSTRATION: Cream card, hand-drawn style. Title "GAIN AFTER COSTS". Line: "£100,000 − £15,000", underline, then "= £85,000" on a green marker highlight.]

**Gain after costs: £85,000.**

### Step 2: take off your annual allowance

You have a £3,000 allowance for 2026/27.

**Taxable gain: £82,000.**

### Step 3: apply the rate

Assume you are a higher-rate taxpayer, so the whole gain is taxed at 24%.

**CGT due: £82,000 × 24% = £19,680.**

[ILLUSTRATION: Cream card. Title "TAX TO PAY". Line: "£82,000 × 24%", underline, then "= £19,680" on a brass or orange marker highlight. Smaller card beside it: "YOU KEEP £80,320 of the profit".]

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price | £300,000 |
| Purchase price | £200,000 |
| Gross gain | £100,000 |
| Less allowable costs | £15,000 |
| Gain after costs | £85,000 |
| Less annual allowance | £3,000 |
| Taxable gain | £82,000 |
| CGT at 24% | £19,680 |
| Profit kept after CGT | £80,320 |

If you were a basic-rate taxpayer with plenty of band left, some of that gain would be taxed at 18% instead, saving you up to £4,920 on this example.

## What counts as a capital improvement?

This is where landlords get caught. HMRC draws a hard line between **improving** a property and **maintaining** it.

| Usually allowable (improvement) | Usually not allowable (maintenance) |
|---|---|
| Extension or loft conversion | Repainting |
| Converting a garage into living space | Replacing carpets like for like |
| A new kitchen or bathroom that improves the property | Fixing a leaking roof |
| Adding something that wasn't there before | General repairs |

Rule of thumb: if it makes the property better than it was, it may count. If it puts it back the way it was, it probably doesn't. Keep every invoice, because HMRC can ask years later.

## Private Residence Relief: your main home

If the property was your main home at any point, Private Residence Relief can wipe out some or all of the gain.

- The time you lived there as your main residence is covered.
- The **final 9 months** of ownership are covered too, even if you had moved out.
- If you let out part of the home, or used part of it only for business, only part of the gain is exempt.

Lettings Relief is now very narrow. Since April 2020 it only helps where you shared the home with your tenant. For most landlords, it won't apply.

## The 60-day rule

If you are UK resident and you owe CGT on a UK residential property, you must **report and pay within 60 days of completion**. You do it online through HMRC's UK property reporting service, separately from your Self Assessment return. You still include the sale on your Self Assessment for the year.

Miss the 60 days and penalties and interest start.

If you are **not** UK resident, the rule is stricter. More on that next.

## Moving to Dubai doesn't make UK CGT disappear

This is the section I wrote this article for.

A lot of people assume that if they move to Dubai first and sell later, the UK can't touch the gain. The UAE has no capital gains tax, so the profit must be tax-free.

On UK property, it isn't.

- **Non-residents pay UK CGT on UK property.** Since April 2015 for residential property, and since April 2019 for all UK land and property.
- **Non-residents must report every UK property sale within 60 days**, even if there is no gain and no tax to pay. There is no "nothing owed, so nothing to file" exception.
- **There is some relief on older gains.** For residential property owned before April 2015, non-residents can usually choose to be taxed only on the gain since 5 April 2015, using a valuation at that date. A UK adviser will tell you which method gives the lowest bill.

### The five-year trap

There is one more rule that catches UK expats. If you were UK resident for at least four of the seven tax years before you left, and you come back to the UK within five years, the **temporary non-residence rules** can tax certain gains you made while abroad, as if you made them in the year you returned.

Assets you bought after leaving are generally outside these rules. But if your plan is "sell the shares in Dubai and come home in two years," talk to an adviser first.

### So what's the smart move for a UK landlord heading to the UAE?

1. **Know your UK CGT number before you list.** Use the steps above, then confirm with a UK accountant.
2. **Plan the timing.** Which tax year you sell in, whether you are resident or non-resident at completion, and how the 60-day filing will work.
3. **Plan the money transfer.** Selling in pounds and buying in dirhams means an FX decision. The dirham is pegged to the US dollar, so you are really choosing your pound to dollar rate.
4. **Put the proceeds to work.** UAE property has no annual property tax and no capital gains tax in the UAE, and Dubai gross rental yields have averaged roughly 6 to 7% in 2026. UK tax on UAE income or gains depends on your residence status, so take advice on that side too. The November 2025 Budget announced that tax rates on **property income** will rise by two points, to 22%, 42% and 47%, from April 2027, and that mortgage interest relief for landlords will become a flat credit at 22%. That's income tax on rent, not CGT. But it changes the hold-or-sell maths for a lot of buy-to-let owners, and it's one reason I'm getting more calls from UK landlords looking at the UAE.

## Five legal ways to reduce the bill

1. **Use both allowances.** Spouses and civil partners can transfer assets between them without a CGT charge. Joint ownership means two £3,000 allowances, and potentially two basic-rate bands.
2. **Use a lower-rate spouse.** If one partner is a basic-rate taxpayer, owning a share in their name can mean more of the gain is taxed at 18% instead of 24%. The transfer must be genuine, with real beneficial ownership.
3. **Time the sale.** Selling early in a tax year, or in a year when your income is lower, can leave more of the basic-rate band available.
4. **Offset losses.** A capital loss on another asset in the same year, or carried forward, can reduce your gain.
5. **Pay into a pension.** Personal pension contributions extend your basic-rate band, which can move part of the gain from 24% to 18%.

None of these is a loophole. They are how the system is designed. Use a qualified adviser to apply them to your situation.

## What about a limited company?

If the property sits in a UK limited company, the company doesn't pay CGT. It pays Corporation Tax on the gain, at 19% to 25% depending on profits. Getting the money out to you personally then has its own tax cost, as dividends or salary. From April 2026, incorporation relief, used when landlords move a portfolio into a company, must be actively claimed rather than applying automatically. Companies are not automatically cheaper. Run both versions.

## Common mistakes

- **No records.** Keep purchase and sale contracts and every improvement invoice.
- **Assuming the main home is always exempt.** Letting it out, or long absences, can make part of the gain taxable.
- **Missing the 60 days.** Especially non-residents, who must file even with nothing to pay.
- **Counting mortgage costs.** Mortgage interest and exit fees reduce your cash, but they are not allowable CGT costs.
- **Forgetting the FX.** If the proceeds are going to Dubai, the exchange rate on the day you move the money can matter as much as the tax.

## Your pre-sale checklist

- [ ] Get an up-to-date valuation.
- [ ] Gather your purchase and sale costs and improvement invoices.
- [ ] Check whether Private Residence Relief applies to any period.
- [ ] Estimate CGT with HMRC's calculator, then confirm with an accountant.
- [ ] Decide timing and ownership structure before you list.
- [ ] If you are or will be non-resident, diary the 60-day report.
- [ ] Set aside the tax in a separate account on completion.
- [ ] Plan the FX and where the money goes next.

## My take

Capital Gains Tax isn't something to fear. It's something to plan.

The difference between keeping 75% of your profit and 80% is usually just records, timing and structure. And if your plan is to move your money from a UK buy-to-let into the UAE, plan both sides of the move together: what you keep in the UK, and what that money can earn in Dubai or Abu Dhabi.

HMRC doesn't care what you made. It cares what you declared. You should care what you keep.
