Look, I know what you're thinking.

*"Why would I pay the developer 1.35 million more when I can grab the same unit on resale for cheaper?"*

On paper, it makes zero sense. In reality, it is all about leverage, and who you are actually going to sell to.

Let me break it down with a real-world style example from Abu Dhabi.

## Same unit, three prices

Two years ago, this unit launched at **AED 7.8 million**.

Today, resale listings are sitting between **AED 7.9 and 8.5 million**.

The developer? They are back at **AED 9.25 million** for the same type of unit.

So resale looks like the deal. But day-one cash tells a completely different story.

## Day-one cash: resale

You buy the cheapest resale at AED 7.9 million. The project is still under construction.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Already paid in by the seller (35% of price), refunded to them | AED 2,765,000 |
| Premium to the seller | AED 100,000 |
| Abu Dhabi registration fee (2%) | AED 158,000 |
| Agency commission (2% + VAT) | AED 165,900 |
| **Day-one cash** | **about AED 3.15 to 3.19 million** |

You are 35% into someone else's payment cycle. You have to give them that money back before you even start.

## Day-one cash: developer direct

Same unit type from the developer at AED 9.25 million.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Down payment (10%) | AED 925,000 |
| Abu Dhabi registration fee (2%) | AED 185,000 |
| Agency commission | none |
| Premium | none |
| **Day-one cash** | **about AED 1.1 million** |

The rest goes on an **interest-free payment plan** through construction.

[CHART: Two bars side by side. Resale: AED 3.15m day-one cash. Developer: AED 1.1m day-one cash. Label the gap: "about AED 2m less cash needed today".]

That is about **AED 2 million less** you need liquid right now, even though the sticker price is higher.

What does AED 2 million of free liquidity do for you? It can be a second unit's down payment. It can sit in a deposit earning 4%. It can be your safety buffer if the market wobbles. Liquidity is optionality.

## The same maths in Dubai

Dubai works the same way, with one big difference: the DLD transfer fee is **4%**, not 2%. That makes resale even heavier on day one.

Take a more typical Dubai apartment. Resale at **AED 2.5 million**, with the seller 30% paid in. The developer is selling the same layout in the next phase at **AED 2.8 million**.

| | Resale at AED 2.5m | Developer at AED 2.8m |
|---|---|---|
| Paid-in amount refunded to seller (30%) | AED 750,000 | none |
| Down payment | none | AED 280,000 (10%) |
| Premium to seller | AED 50,000 | none |
| DLD fee (4%) | AED 100,000 | AED 112,000 |
| Agency (2% + VAT) | AED 52,500 | none |
| **Day-one cash** | **AED 952,500** | **AED 392,000** |

That is about **AED 560,000 less** cash on day one for a unit that costs AED 300,000 more on paper. Some developers also run DLD fee waivers or discounts on launches, which widens the gap further. Always ask.

[CHART: Two bars, Dubai example. Resale AED 952,500 vs developer AED 392,000 day-one cash.]

## Why resale gets stuck before completion

Here is the part most people miss.

Before completion, banks rarely lend on the unit. So when you sell, you are selling to **investors**, paying cash.

And investors do not care about fair value. They care about their margin. They will squeeze you on price, because your profit is literally their discount.

That is why so many off-plan resales trade close to the original launch price, even when the developer has moved its price list way up. In our example, resale at 7.9 to 8.5 versus developer at 9.25. That gap is the investor discount.

## The four phases

Every building goes through four phases, and each phase has a different buyer.

[GRAPHIC: The 4 phases of price appreciation, as a horizontal timeline. Off-plan: investors. Construction: investors. Completion: leveraged buyers. Maturity: end users.]

1. **Off-plan.** Buyers are investors. Cash and payment plans.
2. **Construction.** Still investors. The pool is small and tight.
3. **Completion.** The building is real. Banks lend on it. **Leveraged buyers** arrive, people using mortgages.
4. **Maturity.** **End users**, people who want to live there, drive the price. They are buying a home, not a flip.

Once the building completes, the buyer pool changes completely. Now you have mortgage buyers and end users who can finance the purchase. They are not hunting for a discount. They are looking for a home. That is when you finally get a fair price.

## That's the power of leverage

- **Developers give you time.** The payment plan stretches your cash across construction.
- **Mortgages give end users time.** At completion, a buyer only needs 20% to 50% down.
- **Investors want cash today.** And they will make you pay for that.

So if you buy resale before completion, you are competing in a small, tight pool, with a big cash cheque. If you buy developer direct, you are setting yourself up for the bigger pool at completion, with mortgage buyers and end users who pay market rate.

Same unit. Different cash flow. Different buyer. Different outcome.

## Where interest rates come in

Here is the twist for 2026. Your completion buyer is a mortgage buyer. So their budget depends on interest rates.

The Fed raised rates in September 2026 and the UAE followed the next day. On a AED 1.6 million loan, moving from 3.89% to 5.30% cuts what the same monthly budget can borrow by about **13%**. If rates are higher at your handover, your completion buyer has less to spend. If they come back down, they have more.

## When resale does make sense

I am not anti-resale. It wins when:

- The resale is at or below the **original launch price** and the seller has only paid a small share in.
- The project is **near completion**, so you are close to the leveraged buyer pool anyway.
- The building is **complete**, banks will lend, and you are buying from a motivated seller.
- The developer price list has run far ahead of what the area can support.

Run the day-one cash maths every time. Do not let the sticker price decide for you.

## The risks of buying direct

Developer direct is not free money. Before you sign, be honest about what you are taking on.

- **Delivery risk.** Projects can hand over late. Your payment plan is tied to construction milestones, so delays usually slow payments too, but they also push back your rental income and your exit.
- **Developer risk.** Who is behind the project matters more than the render. Look at their delivery record and how their completed buildings have held value.
- **Price list risk.** A developer's new price is a list price, not a market price. If resale is trading well below it at completion, your exit will price off resale, not the developer.
- **Payment plan stretch.** Post-handover plans feel easy until the post-handover instalments arrive at the same time as service charges. Map every payment on a calendar.

In the UAE, off-plan payments go into a regulated **escrow account** linked to the project, and the sale is registered with the land department. That protection is real. It is not the same as a guarantee on price or timing.

## Five questions before you buy direct

1. What has the developer delivered, and on time?
2. Where does resale for this project trade today, versus the new price list?
3. What is my total cash need, month by month, until handover?
4. If rates are one point higher at completion, what can my mortgage buyer afford?
5. If I want to hold instead of sell, what does it rent for, and does the rent cover a handover mortgage?

If you can answer all five with numbers, you are investing. If not, you are hoping.

## My take

The cheapest price is not always the cheapest deal. In off-plan, cash today, time and the buyer you will sell to matter more than the number on the brochure.

Buy where your cash works hardest. Sell where the biggest pool of buyers is waiting. And respect the rate cycle, because it decides how deep that pool is.
