What strikes me about Venezuelans I meet is not the loss. It is the rebuilding. Engineers who became restaurant owners. Doctors who requalified in a new language. Families who started from zero in a second country, then a third.

I recognise that, because it is my family's story too. I am Jamaican by way of Cuba and Panama, and Saint Lucian by way of Guyana. Caribbean and Latin American people have always moved along these routes, following work and safety. My grandparents' generation went to Panama and Cuba. Now people from the same region are looking at the Gulf.

The UAE is a country built by people from somewhere else. Roughly nine in ten residents are expatriates. Nobody here asks why you left home. They ask what you are building.

## The numbers: why the UAE answers the Venezuelan question

### A currency that does not move

After hyperinflation, the first thing a Venezuelan investor wants to know is: *what is this currency really worth, and will it still be worth that next year?*

The UAE dirham has been pegged to the US dollar at **3.6725** since 1997. The same rate, every day, for nearly thirty years. Property is priced in dirhams, rent is paid in dirhams, and both are effectively dollars.

Many Venezuelan families already think in dollars. The UAE simply lets you hold a dollar-linked asset that pays you rent every month, in a country with no personal income tax.

### Rental income you can live on

Dubai's average gross rental yields have run at roughly **6 to 7%** in 2026. London is closer to 3%. For a family relocating, that matters in two ways:

1. **As an investment:** a well-chosen apartment can generate dollar-linked income while you build a business or career.
2. **As a stepping stone:** many families rent first, learn the city, then buy once they know which community suits them.

Yields have been compressing as prices rise faster than rents. The unit and the building matter more than the city average. I go through my filters in [how I choose off-plan units](/blog/how-i-choose-off-plan-units).

### Safety

For many Venezuelans, safety is the deciding factor. In Numbeo's 2026 safety index, Abu Dhabi ranked first in the world in the January edition and Dubai sat in the global top ten.

When I ask Venezuelan clients what changed after they moved, they talk about small things. Walking at night. Leaving a phone on a café table. Kids riding bikes outside. Those small things are why they stay.

## The practical part: what Venezuelans need to prepare

I am going to be direct here, because this is where most Venezuelan relocations get stuck.

### 1. Your passport

UAE visas need a passport with at least six months' validity. Venezuelan passports have been hard to renew for many people abroad. Sort this first. Everything else depends on it.

### 2. Source of funds

UAE developers, banks and the land department follow strict anti-money-laundering rules. You will be asked where your money came from. That is normal and it protects you.

Prepare bank statements, sale contracts for any property or business you sold, employment records and tax filings from wherever you have lived. If your savings moved through several countries, document each step. Clean paperwork turns a difficult purchase into an easy one.

### 3. Banking

Opening a UAE bank account is easier once you hold residency. Many Venezuelans keep funds in a US, Spanish, Panamanian or Colombian account and wire from there. The developer can accept international transfers directly.

### 4. Costs

Dubai's land department fee is 4% of the price, and Abu Dhabi's registration fee is around 2%. Add agency and admin costs. If you buy off-plan, the payment plan spreads the rest over construction.

## Where Venezuelan families tend to settle

- **Dubai Marina and JLT.** Central, walkable, international, good for a first year.
- **Jumeirah Village Circle and Arjan.** More space for the money, popular with young families, often higher yields.
- **Dubai Hills and Arabian Ranches.** Villas, parks and schools.
- **Abu Dhabi: Yas and Saadiyat.** Calmer, family focused, with long-term infrastructure behind them.

Spanish-speaking communities are growing, with Latin American restaurants, churches, football groups and business networks. You will not be the first.

## The risks, said plainly

- **Cost of living.** Dubai is not cheap. Rent, school fees and health insurance add up. Run your monthly budget before you move.
- **Visa dependence.** Most visas depend on a job, a company or an asset. Plan your route to long-term residency early.
- **Market cycles.** Property prices have risen strongly. They can also soften. Buy for the long term, not the headline.
- **Regional context.** The UAE is very safe day to day, but the wider region has its own politics. Stay informed.
